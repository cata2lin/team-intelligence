# Craft reference — long-form narrative

Deep technique library for `gigi:longform`. Craft is language-agnostic (RO or EN); examples are in EN for clarity.

---

## 1. Hooks — the first 1–3 paragraphs

The opening's only job: make the next sentence irresistible. Promise **tension**, not a topic. Pick one:

| Type | How it works | Mini-example |
|---|---|---|
| **In-media-res scene** | Drop into a specific moment, mid-action | *"At 2 a.m., with the demo forty hours away, the phone still crashed every time someone tapped the browser icon."* |
| **The turn/reversal** | State an expectation, then break it | *"Everyone knew coasting bikes were a joke. Shimano bet $30M they weren't."* |
| **Concrete anomaly** | One strange, true, specific fact | *"The average American who owns a bike hasn't ridden it in eight months."* |
| **Character close-up** | A person, doing something telling | *"Shannon Bryant sells bicycles to people who are afraid of bicycles."* |
| **Stakes/question** | Put what's-at-risk on the table | *"If this didn't work, the whole category — and two careers — were finished."* |

**Kill on sight:** definition openers ("X is a…"), "In today's fast-paced world," throat-clearing throat-clearing ("Before we dive in…"), a summary of what the article will cover.

---

## 2. Structures — outline the ARC, not the subtopics

Pick the shape that fits the story:

- **Chronological narrative** — a thing happened over time; follow it start→crisis→resolution. (iPhone dogfight, product-origin stories.) Strongest default for "how X came to be."
- **Problem → quest → resolution** — a question/obstacle, the pursuit, the payoff (or honest non-payoff). Good for explainers-as-story.
- **Braided / parallel** — two threads (a person + an idea, past + present) that alternate and converge. Adds tension via cross-cutting.
- **Profile arc** — a person as the lens; their change carries the theme. Scene → backstory → turning point → where they are now.
- **Report + analysis** (bikeportland shape) — scene at an event → what was said → your reporting → your critical take → circle-back reflection. Great for shorter (~800w) editorial.

Every section must **push forward** (raise a question, deepen the stakes, turn). If a section only "informs," fold it into a scene or cut it.

---

## 3. Scenes — show, don't summarize

A scene = **place + moment + action + one sharp sensory detail**. One real scene beats three explanatory paragraphs.
- Anchor in time/space: *"In a fluorescent-lit conference room in San Jose…"*
- Give action, not adjectives: *"He slid the prototype across the table"* > *"The prototype was impressive."*
- One concrete detail does the work of ten vague ones. Choose the telling detail (the coffee gone cold, the cracked screen), not a catalog.
- **Reporting inside a scene:** attribute facts to the moment — *"By her own count, they'd interviewed 400 lapsed riders."*

---

## 4. People & quotes — someone must carry the idea

- Ideas are boring in the abstract; give them a **protagonist**. Even a trend piece needs a face.
- **Real quotes only.** Never fabricate a quote or a person. If you don't have one, report the fact plainly or mark it as a gap to source.
- Prefer quotes that reveal character or stakes over quotes that state facts (facts you can narrate yourself).
- Attribution: `"…," said Name, title.` Keep verbs plain ("said"), not "opined/enthused."
- Dialogue creates pace — two people talking moves faster than one paragraph explaining.

---

## 5. Tension & stakes — the engine

- Put **what's at risk** on the table in the first third: money, reputation, a deadline, a belief, a life.
- Keep a **through-line question** open ("Will the demo work?" "Is coasting a real market?"). Don't answer it too early.
- Raise micro-stakes per section; pay the big one at the end.
- Foreshadow lightly ("What no one in the room knew…") — earn it, don't fake it.

---

## 6. Weaving facts / technical info

- Deliver information **on a need-to-know basis**, exactly when the reader must have it to follow the story.
- **Anchor** every fact to a person, scene, or consequence. Not: *"The chip drew 400mW."* But: *"The chip ran so hot it could drain the battery before lunch — and lunch was when Jobs would be on stage."*
- Use an **analogy** for anything technical (bikeportland's dermatologist/Walgreens analogy). One good analogy > a paragraph of spec.
- Never info-dump. If you have a block of exposition, break it across scenes or cut 60% of it.

---

## 7. Voice & rhythm — the sentence-level game

- **Vary sentence length.** Short for impact. Longer, subordinate ones to build and flow, then — a snap. (Read it aloud; monotone length is the #1 tell of flat writing.)
- **Active verbs, concrete nouns.** *"She gutted the spec"* > *"The specification was substantially reduced."*
- **Cut:** adverbs (esp. "very, really, incredibly, truly"), hedges ("somewhat, arguably, it could be said"), empty superlatives ("game-changing, revolutionary"), filler ("in order to," "the fact that").
- **A point of view.** A narrator who notices, judges, connects — not a neutral Wikipedia voice. First person is allowed when it earns intimacy (bikeportland: *"I stood up and asked…"*).
- **Concrete > abstract** always. Name the thing.

---

## 8. Transitions

- Scene-to-scene: a hard cut on a beat, or a bridging image/idea. Don't announce ("Now let's turn to…").
- Time jumps: a short orienting phrase (*"Six months earlier…"*, *"By the time the ship date slipped…"*).
- White space and section breaks are pacing tools — use them.

---

## 9. Kickers — the last 1–2 lines

The ending should resonate, not summarize. Options:
- **Circle back** — return to the opening image/character, now changed. (bikeportland circles to the author's own "I hope to check one out.")
- **Forward window** — where this goes next; the unresolved thread.
- **Resonant image** — end on a concrete picture that carries the theme.
- **The line that reframes** — a final sentence that recasts everything above.
**Never:** "In conclusion," "All in all," or a recap of the article.

---

## 10. Length & pacing

- **~800–1,200w**: report + analysis, single-thread editorial (bikeportland). One scene, one argument, one kicker.
- **1,500–3,000w**: full narrative feature. 3–6 scenes, a clear arc, 2–4 characters.
- **3,000w+**: braided/multi-thread; earn it with genuine complexity.
- Pace by alternating **scene ↔ reflection ↔ scene**. Dense info gets chunked; emotional beats get room.

---

## 11. Worked micro-example (generic → narrative)

**Generic (cut this):**
> *In today's competitive market, launching a new product is challenging. Shimano's Coasting initiative aimed to make cycling more accessible. Through extensive research, they identified key barriers to bike adoption and developed solutions.*

**Narrative (do this):**
> *Shannon Bryant had a problem most bike marketers would kill for and most would fumble: the people she needed to reach didn't think of themselves as cyclists at all. They were the 60 million Americans with a bike in the garage and a story about why they'd stopped. On the closing morning of the National Bike Summit, she walked onstage to explain how Shimano planned to win them back — and why, she admitted, the industry had been asking the wrong question for years.*

Same facts. The second one has a person, a stake, a tension, a scene, and a promise. That's the whole game.

---

## Hard rules
- **Never fabricate** facts, quotes, people, or events. Report what's real; flag gaps to source.
- Run **`gigi:ai-scrub`** before publish (Unicode watermarks + AI-tell phrases, RO blocklist).
- If branded, obey the brand voice (`library:brand` / `shared/apps/<brand>.md`) — narrative craft serves the voice, doesn't override it.
- RO output: the craft is identical; just don't let translation flatten rhythm. Read aloud in Romanian.
